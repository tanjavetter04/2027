## Introduction

While studying Principal Component Analysis (PCA)-based anomaly detection, we came across a widely used library that does not compute what its documentation cites. [PyOD](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod) <d-cite key="zhao2019pyod"></d-cite>, which describes itself as the "most widely used Python library for anomaly detection" <d-cite key="yzhao062"></d-cite>, documents its PCA detector with a reference to the principal component classifier (PCC) of Shyu et al. <d-cite key="shyu2003"></d-cite> and the PCA-based methods described by Aggarwal <d-cite key="aggarwal2017"></d-cite>. However, the anomaly score it computes deviates from the one described in these references. The deviation has been reported publicly since 2019, yet the score has propagated into research, such as the ADBench benchmark <d-cite key="NEURIPS2022_cf93972b"></d-cite>, and into products such as PostHog <d-cite key="posthog"></d-cite>.

Reproducibility does not catch such a case. Pinned versions make results reproducible: the same code yields the same results <d-cite key="nasem2019reproducibility"></d-cite>. Whether the code implements the method it is attributed to is a separate question, known as code verification <d-cite key="roy2005verification"></d-cite>. Published papers are fixed, but the underlying code can change. Existing post-publication mechanisms, such as Crossmark <d-cite key="crossmark"></d-cite> or PubPeer <d-cite key="pubpeer"></d-cite>, attach notes to individual papers, but none links a known deviation in a library to the results that depend on it.

First, we take the case apart. We recap what the cited method computes, show where PyOD deviates from it, and propose a fix. Second, we ask how far the deviating score has spread, why it survived although it has been reported publicly since 2019, and what is missing to make such problems visible. We close with a sketch of correctness advisories that build on existing infrastructure.

## Case study: PCA in PyOD

### What the cited method computes

PCA is a classical method for dimensionality reduction <d-cite key="Jolliffe2002Principal"></d-cite>. It identifies orthogonal directions, the principal components, ordered by how much of the data's variance they explain. The idea behind PCA-based anomaly detection is that observations lying outside the subspace spanned by the major principal components are classified as anomalies. While dimensionality reduction typically retains only the major principal components to capture dominant variance, PCA-based anomaly detection can additionally exploit the minor components. The major components describe the dominant variation of normal data. The minor components have minimal variance and indicate near-linear dependencies among the features, so they are sensitive to observations that break the correlation structure <d-cite key="shyu2003"></d-cite>.

Shyu et al. <d-cite key="shyu2003"></d-cite> build the PCC on this idea. We summarize the parts needed for the anomaly score. The full classifier is described in [Appendix A](#appendix-a). Each feature is first standardized with its sample mean and variance, which moves the centroid of the data to the origin and gives every feature unit variance. We denote a standardized observation by $$\mathbf{z} = (z_1, \dots, z_p)^\top$$.

**Principal components**\
Eigenvalues and eigenvectors are obtained via an eigendecomposition of the $$p \times p$$ correlation matrix $$\mathbf{R}$$. This is the covariance matrix of the standardized values $$\mathbf{z}$$. It yields $$p$$ pairs of eigenvalues and unit eigenvectors $$(\lambda_i, \mathbf{e}_i)$$, ordered such that $$\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots \geq \lambda_p$$.

The key quantity is the $$i$$-th principal component $$y_i$$. It is calculated by projecting each standardized observation $$\mathbf{z}$$ onto the $$i$$-th eigenvector $$\mathbf{e}_i$$:

<p>
$$y_i = \mathbf{e}_i^\top \mathbf{z} = e_{i1} z_1 + e_{i2} z_2 + \dots + e_{ip} z_p $$
</p>

The principal component $$y_i$$ has variance $$\lambda_i$$ on the training data, so $$y_i^2 / \lambda_i$$ represents the squared, variance-normalized distance from the origin along the $$i$$-th principal axis. The sum of $$y_i^2/\lambda_i$$ over all $$p$$ components yields the squared Mahalanobis distance <d-cite key="shyu2003,mahalanobis1936generalized"></d-cite>, which is referred to by Aggarwal <d-cite key="aggarwal2017"></d-cite> as *soft PCA*.

**Major and minor components**\
Instead of summing over all components, Shyu et al. evaluate the first $$q$$ (major) and the last $$r$$ (minor) components with two separate statistics, $$T_{\text{major}}$$ and $$T_{\text{minor}}$$, each a sum of $$y_i^2/\lambda_i$$ over its components ([Appendix A](#appendix-a)). In the minor statistic, the weight $$1/\lambda_i$$ amplifies small deviations that violate the normal correlation structure. An observation is flagged as anomalous if either statistic exceeds its threshold.

### What PyOD computes

We analyze PyOD's PCA detector in version 3.6.8 <d-cite key="pyodPCA368"></d-cite>. It standardizes the data, fits scikit-learn's [PCA](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.decomposition.PCA.html) <d-cite key="scikitlearn"></d-cite>, and scores observations with the explained variance ratios as weights. This pipeline deviates from the PCC in several steps, which we list in [Appendix B](#appendix-b). Most of these deviations are simplifications. The score, however, is a different function.

With the default parameters, PyOD scores a standardized observation $$\mathbf{z}$$ as follows:

<p>
$$
\text{score}_{\text{PyOD}}(\mathbf{z}) = p \sum_{i=1}^{p} \frac{\lVert \mathbf{z} - s_i\, \mathbf{e}_i \rVert}{\lambda_i},
\qquad s_i \in \{-1, +1\}.
$$
</p>

Here $$s_i\mathbf{e}_i$$ are the unit eigenvectors learned from the standardized training data, with a sign $$s_i$$ that scikit-learn sets by convention ([Problem 1](#problem-1)). The numerator is the Euclidean distance from the observation to each eigenvector, treated as a *point* at the tip of the unit vector. This is neither the distance to the principal axis nor the projection $$y_i$$. Each distance is divided by the explained variance ratio $$\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j = \lambda_i / p$$, and the results are summed over all components.

To see what this score does in practice, we first apply it to a small example.

### A short example

Consider $$p = 2$$ standardized features with correlation $$\rho = 0.95$$, so that $$\lambda_1 = 1.95$$, $$\mathbf{e}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1, 1)$$ and $$\lambda_2 = 0.05$$, $$\mathbf{e}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1, -1)$$. In the PCC, $$q = r = 1$$. We score a few observations with the PCC and with PyOD under both signs of $$\mathbf{e}_2$$:

| Observation | Description | PyOD, $$s_2 = +1$$ | PyOD, $$s_2 = -1$$ | PCC ($$T_{\text{major}} + T_{\text{minor}}$$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $$0$$ | training mean | 41.03 | 41.03 | 0.00 |
| $$A = (2, 2)$$ | follows the correlation | 121.88 | 121.88 | 4.10 |
| $$B = (1.5, -1.5)$$ | breaks the correlation | 47.26 | 127.26 | 90.00 |
| $$\mathbf{e}_2$$ | tip of the minor eigenvector | 1.45 | 81.45 | 20.00 |
| $$-\mathbf{e}_2$$ | its mirror image | 81.45 | 1.45 | 20.00 |

For the PCC, $$B$$ is by far the most anomalous observation, and $$A$$ is unremarkable. PyOD with $$s_2 = +1$$ ranks $$A$$ well above $$B$$. Flipping the sign of $$\mathbf{e}_2$$ reverses the order of $$A$$ and $$B$$ and swaps the scores of the two tips. Whichever tip coincides with $$s_2 \mathbf{e}_2$$ even scores lower than the training mean itself.

These observations have a single cause, which we explain first. 

<a id="cause"></a>
### One cause, two problems

PyOD's score sums Euclidean distances to the eigenvector tips $$s_i \mathbf{e}_i$$, all at distance 1 from the center, with weights $$p/\lambda_i$$. Because the weights are inversely proportional to the eigenvalues, the minor components dominate. In the example, the minor component has weight 40 and the major one 1.03, about 97% of the total weight $$W = p\sum_i 1/\lambda_i = 41.03$$, which depends only on the training data. The score is thus essentially

<p>
$$
\text{score}_{\text{PyOD}}(\mathbf{z}) \approx \frac{p}{\lambda_2}\,\lVert \mathbf{z} - s_2\, \mathbf{e}_2 \rVert,
$$
</p>

the Euclidean distance to a single point at distance 1 from the centroid, whose side is set by a sign convention. This explains the whole table. The tip $$s_2 \mathbf{e}_2$$, a clear anomaly for the PCC (20.00), scores 1.45 for PyOD, below the training mean, while its mirror image scores 81.45 ([Problem 1](#problem-1)). And the contours are circles around $$s_2 \mathbf{e}_2$$, so $$A$$, being farther from this point, outranks $$B$$ ([Problem 2](#problem-2)).

One could argue that PyOD's score is a deliberate heuristic and that the cited references were only meant as inspiration. This problem holds regardless: a score that depends on the arbitrary sign of an eigenvector is not a well-defined function of the data.

**In higher dimensions**\
By the triangle inequality, $$W(\lVert \mathbf{z}\rVert - 1) \le \text{score}_{\text{PyOD}}(\mathbf{z}) \le W(\lVert \mathbf{z}\rVert + 1)$$, so the direction of a deviation, including the side set by the sign, shifts the score by at most $$\pm W$$. Since $$\mathbb{E}\lVert \mathbf{z}\rVert^2 = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{R}) = p$$ for standardized data, typical observations have $$\lVert \mathbf{z}\rVert \approx \sqrt{p}$$, so the influence of the direction shrinks like $$1/\sqrt{p}$$ and the score approaches a scaled Euclidean norm. In the two-dimensional example, $$\lVert \mathbf{z}\rVert$$ is of order 1, which is why the single dominant tip decides there.

<a id="problem-1"></a>
### Problem 1: The score depends on an arbitrary sign

PyOD obtains the components from scikit-learn, which computes them via a singular value decomposition of the standardized data. The resulting components are only determined up to their sign. If $$\mathbf{e}_i$$ is a unit eigenvector of $$\mathbf{R}$$, so is $$-\mathbf{e}_i$$. scikit-learn fixes the sign by a convention (`svd_flip`) <d-cite key="sklearnPCA"></d-cite>, which is a purely computational choice and carries no information about the data <d-cite key="broResolvingSignAmbiguity2008"></d-cite>. Expanding the squared norm, with $$\mathbf{e}_i^\top\mathbf{z} = y_i$$ and $$s_i^2 = \lVert\mathbf{e}_i\rVert^2 = 1$$, shows why this matters:

<p>
$$
\lVert \mathbf{z} - s_i \mathbf{e}_i \rVert = \sqrt{\lVert \mathbf{z}\rVert^2 - 2\, s_i\, y_i + 1}.
$$
</p>

The PCC score contains $$y_i^2$$, so the sign of $$\mathbf{e}_i$$ cancels out. In PyOD's $$s_i y_i$$ the sign matters. Flipping it changes the scores and can therefore change the ranking of observations, as in the example above and as already reported in issue #217 <d-cite key="pyodIssue217"></d-cite>. Two observations that deviate equally far along $$\mathbf{e}_i$$, but in opposite directions, are equally anomalous for Shyu et al. For PyOD, the one on the side of the tip looks less anomalous, and which side that is depends on the sign convention, not on the data.

The following figure illustrates this on two correlated features ($$\rho = 0.9$$) with 15 anomalies that break the correlation. With PyOD's score (left), the contours are circles whose center is shifted from the centroid toward the tip $$s_2\mathbf{e}_2$$ of the minor eigenvector, here to the lower right. Anomalies on this side lie close to the tip, so their scores stay below the decision threshold. Anomalies on the opposite side are farther from the tip and are more likely to be detected. Which side is favored depends only on the sign convention. With our fix (right), described [below](#fix), the contours are ellipses centered at the centroid, so anomalies on both sides are treated equally.

**The convention has changed**\
The sign is not only a theoretical concern. Since version 1.5, scikit-learn sets the sign of `components_` based on the component values instead of the transformed data <d-cite key="sklearn15changelog"></d-cite>. On the data of the figure above and with the same PyOD version, the convention before 1.5 flips $$\mathbf{e}_2$$, which shifts the contours to the upper left and lowers ROC AUC from 0.754 to 0.680. On the Wilt dataset of ADBench, ROC AUC is 0.204 with scikit-learn 1.4.2 and 0.402 with scikit-learn 1.6.1 ([How far it spread](#impact)). PyOD's scores therefore depend not only on the PyOD version, but also on the scikit-learn version. The PCC score and our fix are unaffected.

<a id="problem-2"></a>
### Problem 2: The correlation structure is lost

The PCC divides each projection $$y_i$$ by $$\sqrt{\lambda_i}$$ *before* measuring the distance, so a small step along a minor component counts as much as a large step along a major one. PyOD first measures a Euclidean distance, in which all directions count equally, and divides by $$\lambda_i$$ only afterwards, which scales the whole distance regardless of direction. The information about which directions are normal is thus lost before the eigenvalue is applied, although this correlation structure is exactly what PCA is meant to capture. Correlation-breaking anomalies that are not unusually far from the center are therefore hard to detect.

The following figure illustrates this ($$\rho = 0.99$$, standardized). The contours of equal PCC score are ellipses, stretched along the major and squeezed along the minor components. PyOD's terms have circular contours around the eigenvector tips, and away from the center their sum looks like circles around the center. The anomalies in the figure break the correlation but lie close to the center. Their norm is at most 1.6, while 10% of the inliers lie farther out along the major axis. PyOD's threshold is therefore set by these inliers, so all anomalies fall inside it and none is detected. Our fix uses the squared Mahalanobis distance. It normalizes each direction by its eigenvalue before measuring the distance, which yields ellipses along the data and detects 26 of 30 anomalies.

<a id="fix"></a>
### The fix

Our fix adopts the projection proposed in pull request #211 <d-cite key="pyodPR211"></d-cite>, but normalizes by the eigenvalues $$\lambda_i$$ instead of the explained variance ratios:

<p>
$$
\text{score}_{\text{fix}}(\mathbf{z}) = \sum_{i=1}^{p} \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}
$$
</p>

This resolves both problems. Because $$y_i$$ enters the statistic squared, the anomaly score is invariant to the sign $$s_i$$ of the eigenvectors ([Problem 1](#problem-1)). Normalizing each principal direction by its eigenvalue $$\lambda_i$$ ensures that the correlation structure is preserved ([Problem 2](#problem-2)). With the default parameters, the score equals the squared Mahalanobis distance, i.e., Aggarwal's soft PCA. If `n_components` $$= p$$ and `n_selected_components` $$= r$$, the sum runs over the $$r$$ components with the smallest eigenvalues, and the score corresponds to the minor statistic $$T_{\text{minor}}$$ of Shyu et al.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Code change relative to PyOD 3.6.8</summary>

```diff
- self.w_components_ = self.detector_.explained_variance_ratio_
+ self.w_components_ = self.detector_.explained_variance_
  ...
- return np.sum(cdist(X, self.selected_components_)
-               / self.selected_w_components_, axis=1).ravel()
+ projections = (X - self.detector_.mean_) @ self.selected_components_.T
+ return np.sum(projections ** 2 / self.selected_w_components_,
+               axis=1).ravel()
```
</details>

## How far it spread

Until such a fix is merged, every new use of the detector computes the deviating score, and every earlier use already has. PyOD has been downloaded more than 46 million times <d-cite key="yzhao062"></d-cite>. To estimate how far the deviating score has spread, we searched for code that uses the detector and papers that report its results.

### In code

Using the GitHub code search API <d-cite key="githubCodeSearchAPI"></d-cite>, we searched for all import styles of PyOD's PCA, such as `from pyod.models.pca import PCA` or `import pyod.models.pca`. On 10 November 2026, this returned 910 unique files in 525 repositories, excluding forks of PyOD. ### In ADBench

The last entry is the official implementation of ADBench, a benchmark published at NeurIPS 2022 that evaluates 30 algorithms on 57 datasets <d-cite key="NEURIPS2022_cf93972b"></d-cite>. ADBench attributes PCA to Shyu et al., in Figure 2 of the paper (their reference [162]) <d-cite key="NEURIPS2022_cf93972b"></d-cite> and in Appendix B.1 of the supplement <d-cite key="adbenchSupplement"></d-cite>. The appendix describes PCA by the reconstruction error, which matches neither the PCC nor PyOD's score. The code runs PyOD's implementation with default parameters <d-cite key="adbenchPyODWrapper"></d-cite>.

**Reproducing the published numbers**\
ADBench pins `pyod==1.0.0` <d-cite key="adbenchRequirements"></d-cite>, whose scoring code is identical to that of version 3.6.8 <d-cite key="pyodPCA100"></d-cite>, but not scikit-learn, which matters because of [Problem 1](#problem-1). We reran its PCA baseline following the ADBench protocol, with three seeds per dataset (one for the CV and NLP datasets, as in ADBench's code). With scikit-learn 1.4.2, the last release before 1.5, our rerun reproduces the published values <d-cite key="adbenchSupplement"></d-cite> of 54 of the 57 datasets to four decimals. The remaining three, amazon, imdb and yelp, match once we average over three seeds instead of one, as ADBench apparently did for them. With scikit-learn 1.6.1, the same code deviates by up to 0.198 in ROC AUC and fails on six datasets, because the new default solver returns eigenvalues of exactly zero. ADBench's PCA results are therefore reproducible only under a condition its repository does not state. Our fix yields the same results under both versions, so we compare it against PyOD with scikit-learn 1.4.2, i.e., against the published numbers. Protocol and full results are described in the [README of our code](https://anonymous.4open.science/r/iclr_2027_blogpost_pca_code-5E5D/adBench/README.md).

**Effect of the fix**\
Following Demšar <d-cite key="demsar2006statistical"></d-cite>, we compare ROC AUC and average precision (AP) with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test over datasets <d-cite key="wilcoxon1945individual"></d-cite>.

| Metric | PyOD 3.6.8 | Fix | Fix better / worse | $$p$$ |
|---|---:|---:|---|---:|
| ROC AUC | 0.717 | 0.746 | 28 / 29 | 0.13 |
| AP | 0.342 | 0.316 | 27 / 30 | 0.34 |

Neither difference is significant. The higher mean ROC AUC stems from a few datasets with large gains, such as Wilt (+0.45). Per dataset, however, ROC AUC and AP each change by at least 0.05 on 24 of the 57 datasets, several times the standard deviation over seeds (about 0.02).

**Does the deviation matter?**\
Within ADBench, it does. Among the 14 unsupervised algorithms, the fix moves PCA from fifth to first place in average ROC AUC rank (6.33 → 5.07) and to second place in AP. With our own PyOD rerun instead, the rank barely changes (6.40), so the shift comes from the score. Anyone choosing a baseline from ADBench would therefore judge PCA differently, although ADBench's finding that no unsupervised algorithm is statistically better than all others still holds.

**Testing the theory**\
ADBench can also replace the anomalies of its classical datasets with synthetic ones <d-cite key="NEURIPS2022_cf93972b"></d-cite>. [Problem 2](#problem-2) predicts that the fix wins on *dependency* anomalies, which break the correlation without lying far from the center, and that PyOD keeps up on *global* anomalies, which lie far from it. Both predictions hold. On dependency anomalies, ROC AUC rises from 0.652 to 0.892, and the fix is better on all 17 datasets. On global anomalies, both detectors are nearly perfect (0.993 and 0.997). On *cluster* anomalies, however, the fix is clearly worse (0.979 → 0.893). These anomalies are part of the training data, so they stretch the covariance in their own direction and mask themselves. Trained on normal data only, the fix recovers (e.g., from 0.743 to 0.984 on fault). Shyu et al. counter such contamination by trimming the training data, a step that PyOD and our fix omit.

In short, ADBench's PCA results are a correct measurement of PyOD's implementation, reported under a reference whose score they do not compute.

### In papers

ADBench has been cited 865 times <d-cite key="NEURIPS2022_cf93972b"></d-cite> and the PyOD paper 1,484 times <d-cite key="zhao2019pyod"></d-cite>, although not every citing work uses the PCA detector. We found no reliable way to count the papers that use PyOD's PCA implementation, but manually confirmed several examples, such as "Conformal prediction interval for dynamic time-series" (416 citations) <d-cite key="xuConformalPredictionInterval2021"></d-cite>, PIDForest (49 citations) <d-cite key="PIDForestAnomalyDetection2019"></d-cite>, and UADB (24 citations) <d-cite key="yeUADBUnsupervisedAnomaly2023"></d-cite>. All citation counts were retrieved from Google Scholar on 15 November 2026.

Checking the code of a paper only finds direct use. A paper that copies PCA results from a benchmark such as ADBench reports affected numbers without ever importing PyOD, and its repository, if there is one, gives no hint of this. Even the few cases above required reading code repository by repository. There is currently no systematic way to determine which published results rely on this code.

## Why it survived

The deviation did not spread because it was hidden. It has been reported publicly since 2019. This is not meant to blame the PyOD maintainers. A library of this reach is a huge service to the community, and we use it ourselves. Mistakes happen in every codebase, ours included. What interests us is how this one survived for so long. Three observations stand out.

**The documentation cites the method, but the code does not implement it.**\
When the detector was added in commit [a6f05f3](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/commit/a6f05f3be9cddeea2e5a57f6c9c7cbb05efd9723), its docstring already referred to Shyu et al. <d-cite key="shyu2003"></d-cite> and Aggarwal <d-cite key="aggarwal2017"></d-cite>, but also stated the actual computation. One day later, commit [fa7f9fc](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/commit/fa7f9fc948717c0d30ee95a247ae79ea5398f9d5) replaced this formula with a description of a distance to the hyperplane spanned by the eigenvectors, which is closer to the cited methods than to the code. The code itself remained unchanged. Anyone reading the documentation therefore has good reason to assume that the detector implements the cited method. Such drift between comments and code is common <d-cite key="wen2019codecomment"></d-cite>.

**The problem was reported, but not fixed.**\
In 2019, a user observed on their data that the PCA scores correlate with the Euclidean distance to the center ($$r = 0.99$$), which is the high-dimensional regime described [above](#cause). The maintainer closed the issue, attributing this to the data <d-cite key="pyodIssue64"></d-cite>. In 2020, pull request #211 <d-cite key="pyodPR211"></d-cite> implemented the correct projection, and issue #217 <d-cite key="pyodIssue217"></d-cite> pointed out that the ranking depends on the sign of the components, which is [Problem 1](#problem-1). In 2021, pull request #340 <d-cite key="pyodPR340"></d-cite> took up the idea of #217. None of these proposals has been merged to date, and each of them deviates from the cited method in its own way ([Appendix C](#appendix-c)).

**Adoption continued.**\
Although the deviation had been public since 2019, ADBench adopted the detector as a baseline in 2022, along with other unsupervised detectors from PyOD <d-cite key="adbenchPyODWrapper"></d-cite>. Yue Zhao, the first author and maintainer of PyOD <d-cite key="zhao2019pyod"></d-cite>, is also a co-author of ADBench <d-cite key="NEURIPS2022_cf93972b"></d-cite>. Building a benchmark on a library one knows well is a natural choice and keeps the baselines consistent. From there, its results spread into papers that copy benchmark numbers without ever importing PyOD.

Reporting a problem is therefore not enough. The report lives in an issue tracker, while the affected results live in papers, and nothing connects the two.

## What is missing

This case is not unique to PyOD. Software errors have affected published results before. The Willoughby–Hoye scripts for calculating NMR chemical shifts gave different results on different operating systems <d-cite key="bhandarineupane2019leptazolines"></d-cite>. As with PyOD's dependence on scikit-learn's sign convention, the result depended on a convention of the environment, not on the data.

In machine learning, Kriegel et al. <d-cite key="kriegel2017black"></d-cite> show for runtime evaluations that experiments always compare implementations as well as algorithms, and that the implementation alone can dominate the measured differences. Engstrom et al. <d-cite key="engstromImplementationMattersDeep2020"></d-cite> find that undocumented implementation details account for much of the reported advantage of a popular deep reinforcement learning algorithm. Guides to machine learning pitfalls help authors avoid such errors before publication <d-cite key="lones2024pitfalls"></d-cite>. A deviation from the cited method, however, is hard to catch, since it raises no error <d-cite key="tambon2024silent"></d-cite> and the correct output is often unknown <d-cite key="kanewala2014testing"></d-cite>. None of these approaches covers what happens to published results once a deviation becomes known.

A fix in the library does not reach published results, but their pinned versions make it possible to determine whether they are affected. This requires a record of which code and versions are affected, a record of which code and versions a result depends on, and a way to link the two. Existing practices provide only parts of this. Pinned versions record the dependencies, but there is nothing to match them against. Changelogs cover only problems that have been fixed, and they do not link them to specific functions and versions in a machine-readable form. Issue trackers describe open problems, but they are scattered across repositories and not linked to affected versions. Rerunning every result would make matching unnecessary, but it would shift the cost to every reader of every paper.

Mechanisms for correcting the record after publication exist, but they attach to papers, not to code. Crossmark shows readers whether a paper has been corrected or retracted <d-cite key="crossmark"></d-cite>, and standards for retraction notices exist <d-cite key="niso2024crec"></d-cite>. PubPeer hosts comments on individual articles <d-cite key="pubpeer"></d-cite>. All of them require a note on each affected paper, which presupposes that the affected papers are known. The closest counterpart to a correctness advisory is a software registry entry: the Research Resource Identifier (RRID) record of the Willoughby–Hoye scripts now carries a warning that points to the paper describing the bug <d-cite key="rridWilloughbyHoye"></d-cite>. It reaches those who look up the tool, but not the readers of papers that used it. Even data are hard to correct: withdrawn datasets keep circulating through derivatives <d-cite key="peng2021mitigating"></d-cite>.

For security vulnerabilities, this infrastructure exists. CVE started because every tool kept its own, incompatible list of vulnerabilities <d-cite key="mann1999cve"></d-cite>. Today, the OSV schema records affected package versions in a machine-readable format and is exported by GitHub Security Advisories, the PyPI Advisory Database, and others <d-cite key="osvschema"></d-cite>. Tools such as pip-audit <d-cite key="pipAudit"></d-cite> and Dependabot <d-cite key="dependabot"></d-cite> match a project's dependencies against these records automatically, although records alone do not ensure action <d-cite key="kula2018developers"></d-cite>. In security, a published vulnerability is no cause for embarrassment. Known deviations should be treated the same way: what matters is not that they occurred, but that they become visible and get fixed.

## A sketch: correctness advisories

We sketch correctness advisories that link a known deviation in a library to the published results it affects. Coordinated flaw disclosure <d-cite key="cattell2024cfd"></d-cite> and the AI Vulnerability Database <d-cite key="avid"></d-cite> do this for deployed models. We transfer their ideas, together with OSV and VEX, to libraries and published results in four building blocks.

**Provenance**\
Repositories pin exact versions, and papers state for each baseline whether it was computed (library and version) or copied (source) <d-cite key="smith2016software"></d-cite>.

**Advisories**\
An advisory uses the OSV schema <d-cite key="osvschema"></d-cite>, with correctness-specific metadata in its extension fields (e.g., cited method, impact on results, and a status). Affected functions can be listed under `ecosystem_specific` so that matching can check whether a project actually calls them, as the Go vulnerability database does <d-cite key="govulndb"></d-cite>. For PyOD (in YAML for readability):

```yaml
id: MLCA-2026-0001
modified: "2026-11-18T00:00:00Z"
summary: PCA score deviates from the cited methods
affected:
  - package: {ecosystem: PyPI, name: pyod}
    ranges: [{type: ECOSYSTEM, events: [{introduced: "0"}]}]   # no "fixed" event: still open
    ecosystem_specific:
      symbols: [pyod.models.pca.PCA.decision_function]
references:
  - {type: REPORT, url: "https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/issues/64"}   # further: #217, #211, #340
database_specific:
  type: deviation-from-cited-method
  cited_method: [Shyu et al. 2003, Aggarwal 2017]
  impact: ranking          # values | ranking | classification
  status: confirmed        # reported | confirmed | disputed | fixed
```
Since one advisory can affect many results, identified at different times and with varying certainty, each affected result gets its own record. Its status follows VEX <d-cite key="cisa2023vex"></d-cite>, where `not_affected` requires a justification:

```yaml
id: MLCR-2026-0001
advisory: MLCA-2026-0001
result: ADBench (NeurIPS 2022), PCA results in Fig. 4a/c, Fig. 5a–d, Fig. 7a/d, and Appx. D
status: affected       # under_investigation | affected | not_affected (+ justification) | fixed
evidence: pins pyod==1.0.0, scoring code identical to 3.6.8
```
Here, the evidence is the identical scoring code <d-cite key="pyodPCA100"></d-cite>. A record remains `under_investigation` until someone has checked the code and the paper. Maintainers could contribute by marking changes that alter results in their changelogs, as scikit-learn already does <d-cite key="sklearn13changelog"></d-cite>. Such entries translate directly into advisories with a fixed version.

**Matching**\
A tool like pip-audit <d-cite key="pipAudit"></d-cite> checks pinned dependencies against the affected versions and, since versions alone overestimate the impact <d-cite key="pashchenko2022vuln4real"></d-cite>, keeps only repositories that call an affected function <d-cite key="ponta2020detection"></d-cite>. Copied results are matched through provenance statements, not citations alone.

**Visibility**\
Warnings appear where researchers already work: in dependency audits, during review, and in citation managers, as Zotero already does for retracted works <d-cite key="zotero2019retraction"></d-cite>. Unlike a retraction notice, a warning refers to a specific result, not to the paper as a whole.

### Limitations
This sketch leaves several questions open.

**What counts as a deviation**\
Independent implementations of the same algorithm often produce slightly different results <d-cite key="herbold2023differential"></d-cite>, and PyOD's biased standard deviation has little effect. An advisory would therefore need a criterion, e.g., that the deviation changes the ranking on a toy example, as PyOD's PCA score does ([A short example](#a-short-example)). Disagreements between reporters and maintainers are to be expected. Coordinated flaw disclosure handles them with a panel of independent adjudicators that decides when a vendor rejects a report <d-cite key="cattell2024cfd"></d-cite>, and a similar panel could decide whether a deviation is recorded.

**Precision of matching**\
Matching on imports misses models instantiated by name (as in PyCaret), copied source code, and unpublished code. Moreover, existing papers contain no provenance statements. For the existing literature, matching will therefore mostly produce records that require manual review.

**Affected does not mean wrong**\
A confirmed record states that the reported numbers were not obtained with the cited method. It does not state that the conclusions of the paper are wrong. In ADBench, PCA is one of 30 baselines, and the benchmark's main findings still hold (see [How far it spread](#impact)).

**Evaluation**\
Beyond the manual search in [How far it spread](#impact), we have not evaluated whether automated matching is precise enough or whether warnings change how researchers reuse results.

**Governance and misuse**\
Who curates advisories, who hosts them, and why reporters and maintainers would spend time on them remain open questions. Additionally there is the risk that advisories are used against competing work. Requiring reproducible evidence, such as a toy example, and letting maintainers mark an advisory as `disputed` would limit misuse, but not prevent it. Security advisory databases show that such registries can be maintained over many years, but only with an institution behind them.

## Conclusion

PyOD's PCA detector cites Shyu et al., but scores observations by a weighted sum of Euclidean distances to the eigenvector tips instead of by variance-normalized projections. This score depends on the arbitrary sign of each eigenvector and is approximately proportional to the distance from the center. The detector is therefore largely insensitive to anomalies that violate the correlation structure without lying far from the center, which are precisely the anomalies the minor components are meant to detect. Replacing the distances with squared projections normalized by the eigenvalues makes the score sign-invariant and turns it into the squared Mahalanobis distance.

The fix changes the results in both directions. On ADBench, ROC AUC and AP change substantially in both directions depending on the dataset, and neither average difference is significant. The changes follow the theory, however. The fix detects anomalies that break the correlation structure, which PyOD's score largely misses, and its ROC AUC drops where anomalies in the training data distort the covariance. Within ADBench, it changes the ranking of PCA among the unsupervised algorithms. Our main finding is therefore not a performance gain. It is that the PCA results reported in ADBench and in papers such as PIDForest and UADB, as well as in 525 repositories that use the detector, were obtained with a score other than the one described in the cited references, although the deviation has been reported since 2019.

The code behind these results is not verified, and, as ADBench shows, reproducing them can depend on versions that were never pinned. This does not invalidate the conclusions of the affected papers. It only means that their PCA numbers come from PyOD's score, not from the method its documentation cites. Fixing the detector does not reach these results, and nothing currently connects a known deviation to the results it affects. Beyond this case, we sketched how known deviations could be made as visible as known vulnerabilities, largely by combining existing pieces: coordinated flaw disclosure, advisories modeled on OSV, VEX-style records for affected results, provenance statements in papers, and warnings in tools researchers already use.

### Future work
Our fix deliberately changes only the score, so that its effect on ADBench can be attributed to this single component. The remaining parts of the PCC, namely trimming, separate major and minor statistics, and two thresholds, are not implemented in PyOD. Such components can substantially change the behavior of a method. A natural next step is to implement the full PCC as a separate detector class and to evaluate its components one at a time. This requires an additional design decision, because the PCC is a classifier with two thresholds, whereas benchmarks such as ADBench evaluate a single continuous score.

For the sketch, governance, the exact status workflow, and automated matching remain open design questions. Lessons from establishing CVE are a starting point <d-cite key="baker1999cve"></d-cite>.

### Final thoughts

Popularity is not a proof of correctness <d-cite key="soergel2015rampant"></d-cite>. Wide use is often taken as a sign that many people have checked the code. In this case, many users and several public reports did not lead to a fix. In our view, wide use therefore says little about whether anyone has compared the code with the method it cites.

Comparing an implementation with its documentation or the paper it cites is a tedious task where LLMs can help, although current models still miss more than half of real paper–code discrepancies <d-cite key="baumgartner2026scicoqa"></d-cite>. As Shah <d-cite key="researchAskingAuthorsTheir2026"></d-cite> reminds us, authors are responsible for the accuracy and integrity of the papers they submit under their name. We think this responsibility extends to the tools a paper builds on.

Advisories help once a problem is known. Finding it in the first place still depends on individual researchers. We therefore recommend: before you report results for a method, check that its implementation does what the paper says. Read the scoring function and look at the open issues. An LLM can do a first pass. Then test the implementation on a toy example where you know the answer.

## Appendix A: The full principal component classifier

**Standardization**\
Since the features are measured on different scales, features with large numerical ranges would dominate the principal components. Each feature $$k$$ is therefore standardized using its sample mean and sample variance over all $$n$$ training observations $$j$$:

<p>
$$
\bar{x}_k = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} x_{jk}, \qquad
s_{kk} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \left(x_{jk} - \bar{x}_k\right)^2
$$
</p>

The factor $$\frac{1}{n-1}$$ yields an unbiased variance estimate, since the mean itself is estimated from the same data. The standardized observation $$\mathbf{z}_j$$ is then

<p>
$$
z_{jk} = \frac{x_{jk} - \bar{x}_k}{\sqrt{s_{kk}}}, \qquad
\mathbf{z}_j = (z_{j1}, z_{j2}, \dots, z_{jp})^\top
$$
</p>

**Trimming**\
To make the PCC more robust against anomalies in the training data, the $$\gamma \cdot n$$ observations with the largest Mahalanobis distances <d-cite key="mahalanobis1936generalized"></d-cite> are removed, as they might represent extreme values (e.g., $$\gamma=0.005$$ in Shyu et al. <d-cite key="shyu2003"></d-cite>). Afterwards, mean and correlation matrix are reestimated from the remaining observations.

**Major and minor components**\
Instead of summing over all components, Shyu et al. evaluate the major and minor components using two separate statistics.

- The major statistic sums over the first $$q$$ components, where $$q$$ is the smallest number of components that together explain more than $$50\%$$ of the total variance. It detects observations with extreme values along the dominant directions.

  <p>
  $$T_{\text{major}}(\mathbf{z}) = \sum_{i=1}^{q} \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}.$$
  </p>

- The minor statistic sums over the last $$r$$ components with eigenvalues $$\lambda_i < 0.20$$ (of the correlation matrix), where the weight $$1/\lambda_i$$ amplifies small deviations that violate the normal correlation structure.

  <p>
  $$T_{\text{minor}}(\mathbf{z}) = \sum_{i=p-r+1}^{p} \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}.$$
  </p>

The minor statistic resembles Aggarwal's *hard PCA* <d-cite key="aggarwal2017"></d-cite>, which scores observations only along low-variance components. By pairing it with $$T_{\text{major}}(\mathbf{z})$$, the PCC also captures extreme values along the dominant directions that hard PCA misses. In contrast to a single combined score, the two statistics also indicate which type of anomaly an observation represents.

**Classification**\
To classify an unseen observation $$\mathbf{z}$$, anomaly thresholds $$c_1$$ and $$c_2$$ are defined for the major and minor statistics. An observation is flagged as anomalous if either threshold is exceeded:

<p>
$$T_{\text{major}}(\mathbf{z}) > c_1 \quad \text{or} \quad T_{\text{minor}}(\mathbf{z}) > c_2 .$$
</p>

The thresholds $$c_1$$ and $$c_2$$ are the $$(1 - \alpha_1)$$- and $$(1 - \alpha_2)$$-quantiles of the empirical distributions of the two statistics on the training data, chosen according to individual false alarm rates $$\alpha_1$$ and $$\alpha_2$$ to achieve an overall false alarm rate of $$\alpha = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 - \alpha_1 \alpha_2$$, assuming that the two statistics are independent.

## Appendix B: Differences between the PCC and PyOD

| Step | Shyu et al. | PyOD (defaults) | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standardization | sample std. (`ddof=1`) | `StandardScaler` (`ddof=0`) | negligible |
| Decomposition | eigendecomposition of $$\mathbf{R}$$ | SVD of the standardized data | identical up to the sign of each component |
| Trimming | remove the $$\gamma \cdot n$$ most extreme observations ($$\gamma = 0.005$$) | none | less robust to anomalies in the training data |
| Components | $$q$$ major and $$r$$ minor, evaluated separately | all $$p$$ components in a single sum | major and minor anomalies are not distinguished |
| Threshold | $$c_1$$, $$c_2$$ from false alarm rates $$\alpha_1$$, $$\alpha_2$$ | one quantile of the training scores (`contamination=0.1`) | a single threshold |
| Score | $$\sum_i \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}$$ per statistic | $$p \sum_i \frac{\lVert \mathbf{z} - s_i \mathbf{e}_i \rVert}{\lambda_i}$$ | different function (see [What PyOD computes](#what-pyod-computes)) |

**Standardization**\
The `StandardScaler` <d-cite key="standardscaler"></d-cite> divides by the biased standard deviation (`ddof=0`), which scikit-learn considers to have little effect on model performance.

**Decomposition**\
PyOD uses scikit-learn, which computes the components via a singular value decomposition of the standardized data instead of an eigendecomposition of the correlation matrix. Both yield the same components up to their sign, which scikit-learn fixes by convention (`svd_flip`) <d-cite key="sklearnPCA"></d-cite>. For the PCC score this is irrelevant, because $$y_i$$ enters the statistic squared. For PyOD's score it is not, as shown in [Problem 1](#problem-1).

**Trimming**\
PyOD fits the PCA on all training observations. Anomalies in nominally normal training data can inflate the estimated variances and rotate the components <d-cite key="shyu2003"></d-cite>, so that anomalies partly become part of the learned normal subspace <d-cite key="ringbergSensitivityPCATraffic2007a"></d-cite>. Shyu et al. prevent this by trimming.

**Component selection**\
By default, PyOD uses all $$p$$ components. If `n_components` is set to $$k < p$$, only the $$k$$ components with the largest variance are retained. `n_selected_components` then selects the *last* of these for scoring. They correspond to minor components in the sense of Shyu et al. only if $$k = p$$. PyOD provides no option to evaluate major and minor components separately.

## Appendix C: Scores proposed in earlier reports

In 2020, pull request #211 <d-cite key="pyodPR211"></d-cite> implemented the correct projection, but normalized the squared scores by the explained variance ratio instead of the eigenvalue $$\lambda_i$$. This scales all scores by the total variance $$\sum_j \lambda_j$$, preserving the exact Mahalanobis ranking while changing the numerical values. With the default `whiten=False`, the proposal in issue #217 <d-cite key="pyodIssue217"></d-cite> is equivalent to #211. Enabling `whiten=True` causes `fit_transform` to return already normalized scores $$t_i = y_i / \sqrt{\lambda_i}$$, so each component is weighted by $$1/\lambda_i^2$$ instead of $$1/\lambda_i$$.

In 2021, pull request #340 <d-cite key="pyodPR340"></d-cite> took up the idea of #217, but centers with the mean of the current batch instead of the training mean. As a result, a test observation scored on its own always gets a score of 0. In addition, the weight is placed inside the square, so the components are weighted by $$1/\lambda_i^2$$ instead of $$1/\lambda_i$$.

The following table summarizes the scores.

| Version | Score |
|---|---|
| current PyOD | $$\sum_i \frac{\lVert \mathbf{z} - s_i \mathbf{e}_i \rVert}{\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j}$$ |
| [#211](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/pull/211) | $$\sum_i \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j}$$|
| [#217](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/issues/217) | $$\sum_i \frac{t_i^2}{\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j}$$|
| [#340](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/pull/340) | $$\sum_i \left(\frac{t_i - \bar{t}_i^{\text{batch}}}{\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j}\right)^2$$ |
| ours | $$\sum_i \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}$$ |

where $$y_i = \mathbf{e}_i^\top \mathbf{z}$$ denotes the projection of the standardized observation $$\mathbf{z}$$ onto eigenvector $$\mathbf{e}_i$$, and $$t_i$$ denotes the output of scikit-learn's transform ($$t_i = y_i$$ for `whiten=False`, and $$t_i = y_i / \sqrt{\lambda_i}$$ for `whiten=True`).
