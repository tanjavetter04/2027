# PCA
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a commonly used tool for dimensionality reduction. It finds orthogonal directions, the principal components, ordered by how much of the data's variance they explain. For dimensionality reduction, one keeps the first few components and discards the rest. For anomaly detection, both ends are informative: the first components describe the dominant variation in normal data, and the last components describe relationships between features that normal data almost never violate.

Shyu et al. <d-cite key="shyuPrincipalComponentbasedAnomaly2005"></d-cite> describes a principle component classifier for outlier detection like the follows.

**Standardization**\
Features can live on very different scales, so each feature $k$ is standardized with the sample mean and the sample variance:

$$
\bar{x}_k = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} x_{jk}, \qquad
s_{kk} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \left(x_{jk} - \bar{x}_k\right)^2, \qquad
z_k = \frac{x_k - \bar{x}_k}{\sqrt{s_{kk}}}.
$$

The factor $1/(n-1)$ (ddof=1) makes $s_{kk}$ an unbiased estimator of the variance, because the true mean is unknown and has to be estimated from the same data.

**Trimming**\
To make the PCC more robust the $\gamma \cdot n$ observations with the largest Mahalanobis distances are removed, as they might be extreme values. In the paper they used $\gamma=0.005$ for their experiments. Mean and covariance are estimated again.

**Principle components**\
Eigenvalues and eigenvectors are calculated via eigenvalue decomposition from the covariance matrix $R$. This is the $p \times p$-matrix of the standardised values $\mathbf{z}$. The result from the eigenvalue decomposition are $p$ pairs of the eigenvalue and unit eigenvector $(\lambda_i, \mathbf{e}_i)$.

Important for anomaly detection is the principal component score. This is calculated by projecting each datapoint (standardised) onto the principal components by

$$y_i = e_i^\top z = e_{i1} z_1 + e_{i2} z_2 + \dots + e_{ip} z_p .$$

The score $y_i$ has variance $\lambda_i$, so $y_i^2 / \lambda_i$ is the squared, variance-normalized distance from the center along component $i$. Aggarwal <d-cite key="aggarwalOutlierAnalysis2017"></d-cite> calls the sum of $y_i^2/\lambda_i$ over all components *soft PCA*.

**Major and minor components**\
Instead of summing over all components, Shyu et al. use two separate statistics. 

- The major components are the first $q$ components, that together explain more than 50% of the total variance. This is used to detect anomalies, with an extreme values on original features.

  $$T_{\text{major}}(z) = \sum_{i=1}^{q} \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}.$$

- The minor components on the other hand is used to find anomalies which do not follow the correlation structure of the normal data. These are the $r$ components selected by $\lambda_i < 0.20$ (so basically linear dependencies):

  $$T_{\text{minor}}(z) = \sum_{i=p-r+1}^{p} \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}.$$

  The factor $1/\lambda_i$ amplifies even small deviations along such directions.

The minor statistic resembles Aggarwal's hard PCA, which scores a sample only by its deviation along the low-variance components. Hard PCA misses extreme values along the dominant directions, which the major components dow.

**Classification**\
To detect only anomalies an outlier threshold needs to be defined. An observation is flagged as anomalous if

$$T_{\text{major}}(z) > c_1 \quad \text{or} \quad T_{\text{minor}}(z) > c_2 .$$

The thresholds $c_1$ and $c_2$ are quantiles of the empirical distributions of the two statistics on the training data, chosen for target false alarm rates $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$. Which is defined as $\alpha = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 - \alpha_1 \alpha_2 .$ Unlike a single score, the two statistics also show which kind of anomaly a sample is.

## Implementation of PCA in PyOD

PyOD's PCA detector standardizes the data, fits scikit-learn's PCA, and scores samples with the explained variance ratios as weights. Compared to the principal component classifier of Shyu et al., the pipeline deviates in several places.

### PyOD's difference as overview

| Step | Shyu et al. | PyOD (defaults) | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standardization | sample std. (`ddof=1`) | `StandardScaler` (`ddof=0`) | negligible |
| Decomposition | eigendecomposition of $R$ | SVD of the standardized data | equivalent up to the sign of each component |
| Trimming | remove the $\gamma n$ most extreme observations ($\gamma = 0.005$) | none | less robust to extreme values in the training data |
| Components | $q$ major (≥ 50% variance) and $r$ minor ($\lambda_i < 0.20$), evaluated separately | all components in a single sum | major and minor anomalies are no longer distinguished |
| Threshold | $c_1$, $c_2$ from false alarm rates $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$ | one quantile of the training scores (`contamination=0.1`) | one threshold instead of two |
| Score | $\sum y_i^2 / \lambda_i$ | $\sum \lVert z - s_i e_i \rVert \,/\, (\lambda_i / p)$ | different function (see below) |

**Standardization**\
Standardization is performed with the [`StandardScaler`](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.preprocessing.StandardScaler.html), which is a biased estimator with `ddof=0`. The documenation of the `StandardScaler` notes that the choice of ddof is unlikely to effect the models performance.

**Decomposition**
scikit-learn, which is used by PcOD, computes the components via a singular value decomposition of the centered data instead of an eigendecomposition of the correlation matrix. This results in the same components, however, only up to their sign. For Shyu's score this is irrelevant, because $y_i$ enters squared. For PyOD's score it is not, as we show below.

**Trimming**\
PyOD fits on all training observations. Extreme values in nominally normal training data can inflate the estimated variances and tilt the components, which Shyu et al. prevent by trimming.

**Component Selection**\
By default, PyOD keeps all $p$ components and sums over all of them. If `n_components` is set, only the components with the largest variance are kept. `n_selected_components` then selects the *last* of these kept components for scoring. These compare to Shyu et al. minor components only if all components were kept. There is no option to evaluate major and minor components separately, like it was done by Shyu et al.

**Threshold.** 
PyOD sets a single threshold at the $(1 - \text{contamination})$ quantile of the training scores, with `contamination=0.1` by default. Shyu et al. use two thresholds one for major and one for the minor components.

### Score Calculation

PyOD's documentation cites Shyu et al. <d-cite key="shyuPrincipalComponentbasedAnomaly2005"></d-cite> and Aggarwal <d-cite key="Aggarwal2013"></d-cite> for its PCA detector. The pipeline differences above could be seen as simplifications, but the score itself is a different function. It depends on an arbitrary sign convention, and it ranks points mostly by their distance to the center of the data, ignoring the correlation structure that PCA is meant to capture.

#### Computation

Using the default parameters, the score in PyOD is implemented as
  
```python
np.sum(cdist(X, self.selected_components_) / self.selected_w_components_, axis=1)
```
where `X` is the standardized input, `selected_components_` contains all $p$ unit eigenvectors as rows, and `selected_w_components_` contains the explained variance ratios. In the notation introduced above, the formula of the score is

$$
\text{score}_{\text{PyOD}}(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{p} \frac{\lVert z - s_i\, e_i \rVert}{\lambda_i / p}
= p \sum_{i=1}^{p} \frac{\lVert z - s_i\, e_i \rVert}{\lambda_i},
\qquad s_i \in \{-1, +1\}
$$

where $s_i$ is the sign that `svd_flip` assigns to the $i$-th component. Each term is the Euclidean distance from the sample to the *tip* of an eigenvector, divided by its weight. In the  original code comments in commit [a6f05f3b](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/commit/a6f05f3be9cddeea2e5a57f6c9c7cbb05efd9723) this is described, but since then the comment changed without the code to change. Now the comments refer <d-cite key="shyuPrincipalComponentbasedAnomaly2005"></d-cite> and Aggarwal <d-cite key="Aggarwal2013"></d-cite>. The actual score differs from it in two fundamental ways.

<a id="problem-1"></a>
#### Problem 1: The sign

Expanding the numerator of the PyOD score (see Appendix 1) gives
 
$$
\lVert z - s_i e_i \rVert = \sqrt{\lVert z\rVert^2 - 2\, s_i\, y_i + 1}.
$$

Shyu's score contains $y_i^2$, so the sign of $e_i$ cancels out. PyOD's score contains $s_i y_i$, so the sign matters.  Flipping it changes the scores and can change the ranking of samples, as already reported in [issue #217](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/issues/217).

**Meaning for anomalies**\
Each term of PyOD's score measures how far a sample is from the tip of an eigenvector, $s_i e_i$, and this tip lies on only one side of the center. Consider two samples that deviate from the center by the same amount along $e_i$, but in opposite directions. The sample on the side of the tip moves closer to it, so its term shrinks. The sample on the other side moves away from it, so its term grows. For Shyu et al., both samples are equally anomalous. For PyOD, one of them looks less anomalous, and which one is decided by the sign convention, not by the data.

The effect is strongest for the minor components, because their terms carry the largest weights $p/\lambda_i$. In the extreme case, a sample lies exactly at the tip, $z = s_i e_i$. For Shyu et al., this is a clear anomaly with score $1/\lambda_i$. For PyOD, its distance to the tip is zero, so exactly the term with the largest weight vanishes.

<a id="problem-2"></a>
#### Problem 2: The score is close to the norm of $z$
Each term of the score is the distance from $z$ to an eigenvector tip $s_i e_i$, multiplied by the weight $p/\lambda_i$. All tips lie at distance 1 from the center, because they are unit vectors. The distance from $z$ to a tip is therefore almost the same as the distance from $z$ to the center: by the triangle inequality, it is at least $\lVert z\rVert - 1$ and at most $\lVert z\rVert + 1$. Every term is thus roughly $\lVert z\rVert$ times its weight, and the whole score is roughly

$$
\text{score}_{\text{PyOD}}(z) \approx W \cdot \lVert z\rVert, \qquad W = p\sum_{i=1}^{p} \frac{1}{\lambda_i}.
$$

$W$ depends only on the eigenvalues of the training data and is the same for every sample. The score is therefore essentially the distance to the center, multiplied by a constant. The direction in which a sample deviates can shift its score by at most $\pm W$, i.e. by as much as one unit of norm would.

**Eigenvalue problematic**\
The key difference lies in when the eigenvalues enter the score. Shyu et al. first scale each direction separately and then measure the distance, so each component score $y_i$ is divided by $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. That means a small step along a minor component counts as much as a large step along a major one. PyOD does it the other way round. It first computes the Euclidean distance to the tip $s_i e_i$, in which all directions count equally, and only afterwards divides by $\lambda_i$. This division scales the whole distance by the same factor, no matter in which direction the sample deviates. The information about which directions are normal is therefore lost before the eigenvalue is even applied.

The norm ignores how the features are correlated, which is exactly the structure PCA is meant to capture. Correlation-breaking anomalies that are not unusually far from the center are therefore hard to detect. The effect grows with the dimension: typical samples have $\lVert z\rVert \approx \sqrt{p}$, so the tolerance of $\pm 1$ shrinks relative to the norm. This matches issue [issue #64](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/issues/64), where the PCA scores correlated at 0.99 with the distance to the origin. 

Geometrically speaking, Shyu's contours of equal score are ellipses, stretched along the major and squeezed along the minor components. PyOD's terms have circular contours around the eigenvector tips, which treat all directions alike. Away from the center, their sum looks like circles around the center.

#### A short example
 
Consider $p = 2$ standardized sensors with correlation $\rho = 0.95$, so that $\lambda_1 = 1.95$, $e_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1, 1)$ and $\lambda_2 = 0.05$, $e_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1, -1)$. In Shyu's classifier, $q = r = 1$. We score a few samples with Shyu's statistics and with PyOD under both signs of $e_2$:
 
| Sample | | Shyu | PyOD, $s_2 = +1$ | PyOD, $s_2 = -1$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | training mean | 0.00 | 41.03 | 41.03 |
| $A = (2, 2)$ | follows the correlation | 4.10 | 121.88 | 121.88 |
| $B = (1.5, -1.5)$ | breaks the correlation | 90.00 | 47.26 | 127.26 |
| $e_2$ | tip of the minor eigenvector | 20.00 | 1.45 | 81.45 |
| $-e_2$ | its mirror image | 20.00 | 81.45 | 1.45 |
 
For Shyu et al., $B$ is by far the most anomalous sample, and $A$ is unremarkable. PyOD with $s_2 = +1$ ranks $A$ well above $B$. Flipping the sign of $e_2$ reverses the order of $A$ and $B$ and swaps the scores of the two tips. Whichever tip coincides with $s_2 e_2$ scores lower than the training mean itself.

## Community found error

- [Issue 64](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/issues/64) (2019): a user observed on his data that the PCA score correlates with the euclidean distance to the origin ($$r = 0.99$$). The maintainer closed the issue, attributing this to the data.
- In 2020, [Pull Request 211](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/pull/211) implemented the correct projection and [Issue 217](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/issues/217) described the problem. Since #211 divides by the explained variance ratio instead of the eigenvalue, all its scores are scaled by the same factor, the total variance $$\sum_j \lambda_j$$. The ranking is identical to the Mahalanobis distance, but the values are not. With the default `whiten=False`, the proposal in #217 is equivalent to #211. With `whiten=True`, `fit_transform` already returns $$t_i = y_i / \sqrt{\lambda_i}$$, so each component is weighted by $$1/\lambda_i^2$$ instead of $$1/\lambda_i$$. Both are still open and have not been merged.
- In 2021, [Pull Request 340](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/pull/340) took up the idea of #217, but centers with the mean of the current batch instead of the training mean. As a result, a test sample scored on its own always gets a score of 0. In addition, the weight is placed inside the square, so the components are weighted by $$1/\lambda_i^2$$ instead of $$1/\lambda_i$$. This pull request is also still open.

| | score | code |
|---|---|---|
| current PyOD | $$\sum_i \lVert z - e_i \rVert \,/\, (\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j)$$ | `cdist(X, C) / ratio` |
| [#211](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/pull/211) | $$\sum_i y_i^2 \,/\, (\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j)$$ | `((X - mean) @ C.T)**2 / ratio` |
| [#217](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/issues/217) | $$\sum_i t_i^2 \,/\, (\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j)$$ | `fit_transform(X)**2 / ratio` |
| [#340](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/pull/340) | $$\sum_i \big((t_i - \bar t_i^{\text{batch}}) \,/\, (\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j)\big)^2$$ | `((T - T.mean(0)) / ratio)**2` |
| ours | $$\sum_i y_i^2 / \lambda_i$$ | `((X - mean) @ C.T)**2 / var` |

with $$y_i = e_i^\top (z - \bar z)$$ the projection onto eigenvector $$e_i$$ and $$t_i$$ the output of `transform`.

## Suggested Fix
The correct score was already proposed in 2020 in [Pull Request 211](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/pull/211), which has remained open since. Our suggested fix builds on this proposal. Instead of the distance to the tips of the eigenvectors, we calculate the projection onto the eigenvectors (see soft PCA score in the [PCA](#pca) section). With `n_selected_components`, the sum runs only over the last components, which corresponds to the minor statistic of Shyu et al.
```python
# before (pyod 3.6.7)
self.w_components_ = self.detector_.explained_variance_ratio_
...
return np.sum(cdist(X, self.selected_components_) / self.selected_w_components_, axis=1).ravel()

# after
self.w_components_ = self.detector_.explained_variance_
...
projections = (X - self.detector_.mean_) @ self.selected_components_.T
return np.sum(projections ** 2 / self.selected_w_components_, axis=1).ravel()
```
This addresses both problems mentioned above. Since $y_i$ enters squares, the sign of the eigenvectors does not matter ([Problem 1](#problem-1)). Since each direction is scaled by its own variance before the distance is taken, the correlation structure is preserved ([Problem 2](#problem-2)).

## Appendix 1

Norm as a dot product

$$\lVert z - s_i e_i \rVert^2 = (z - s_i e_i)'(z - s_i e_i)$$

Expand it, and as the dot product is symmetric, we can combine the two middle terms

$$(z - s_i e_i)'(z - s_i e_i) = z'z \;-\; s_i\, z'e_i \;-\; s_i\, e_i'z \;+\; s_i^2\, e_i'e_i = z'z \;-\; 2\, s_i\, e_i'z \;+\; s_i^2\, e_i'e_i$$

Now we substitute

| Term | Value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| $z'z$ | $\lVert z \rVert^2$ | definition of the norm |
| $e_i'z$ | $y_i$ | Shyu et al., Eq. (4): $y_i = e_i'z$ |
| $s_i^2$ | $1$ | $s_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ |
| $e_i'e_i$ | $1$ | eigenvectors have unit length (`components_` is orthonormal) |

This gives:

$$\lVert z - s_i e_i \rVert^2 = \lVert z \rVert^2 - 2\,s_i\,y_i + 1$$

Take the square root

$$\lVert z - s_i e_i \rVert = \sqrt{\lVert z \rVert^2 - 2\,s_i\,y_i + 1}$$
