## Introduction

Every day, we build on code we have never read. When we choose a library, we skim its documentation, check that others use it, and move on. Few researchers read its source code in detail. Generative AI widens this gap, as more code and text are produced without anyone working through every detail. Shah <d-cite key="researchAskingAuthorsTheir2026"></d-cite> reports that papers are being submitted that the authors cannot explain and that appear to be AI-generated. If authors do not fully understand their papers, it is even less likely that the underlying libraries are checked for errors.

We came across one such case while studying Principal Component Analysis (PCA)-based anomaly detection with [PyOD](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod) <d-cite key="zhao2019pyod"></d-cite>. It documents its PCA detector with a reference to the principal component classifier (PCC) of Shyu et al. <d-cite key="shyu2003"></d-cite> and the PCA-based methods described by Aggarwal <d-cite key="aggarwal2017"></d-cite>. However, the calculation of the anomaly score implemented in PyOD deviates from the one described in these references. With PyOD describing itself as the most widely used Python library for anomaly detection <d-cite key="yzhao062"></d-cite>, the deviating score has propagated into research, such as the ADBench benchmark <d-cite key="NEURIPS2022_cf93972b"></d-cite> and into products such as PostHog <d-cite key="posthog"></d-cite>.

This deviating implementation shows us a structural problem. Pinned versions do make results reproducible, but they don't make them correct. If you run ADBench today with the same versions as the original paper, you will get the same results with the same deviating anomaly score. Published paper are fixed, but the underlying code can change. At the moment, there isn't a mechanism to inform authors, reviewers and readers that the code is not implemented as described in the references.

In the following, we recap the PCA for anomaly detection as described in the literature, show where PyOD deviates from it, trace earlier community reports of the problem, and propose a fix together with its impact. Additionally, we propose a system for the community to make such problems visible in the future.

## Principal Component Classifier (PCC)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a classical method for dimensionality reduction <d-cite key="Jolliffe2002Principal"></d-cite>. It identifies orthogonal directions, the principal components, ordered by how much of the data's variance they explain. The idea using PCA for outlier detection is, that data outside the described reduced area described by the principal compontents, is classified as outlier. While dimensionality reduction typically retains only the major principal components to capture dominant variance, PCA-based anomaly detection can additionally exploit the minor components. The major components describe the dominant variation of normal data. The minor components have minimal variance and indicate near-linear dependencies among the features, so they are sensitive to observations that break the correlation structure <d-cite key="shyu2003"></d-cite>.

Shyu et al. <d-cite key="shyu2003"></d-cite> describe a principal component classifier for anomaly detection as follows.

**Standardization**\
Since the features are measured on different scales, features with large numerical ranges would dominate the principal components. Each feature $$k$$ is therefore standardized using its sample mean and sample variance over all $$n$$ time steps $$j$$:

<p>
$$
\bar{x}_k = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} x_{jk}, \qquad
s_{kk} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \left(x_{jk} - \bar{x}_k\right)^2
$$
</p>

The factor $$\frac{1}{n-1}$$ yields an unbiased variance estimate, since the mean itself is estimated from the same data. The standardized observation $$\mathbf{z}_j$$ is then

<p>
$$
z_{jk} = \frac{x_{jk} - \bar{x}_k}{\sqrt{s_{kk}}}, \qquad
\mathbf{z}_j = (z_{j1}, z_{j2}, \dots, z_{jp})^\top
$$
</p>

This moves the centroid of the data to the origin and gives every feature unit variance.

**Trimming**\
To make the PCC more robust against outliers in the training data, the $$\gamma \cdot n$$ observations with the largest Mahalanobis distances <d-cite key="mahalanobis1936generalized"></d-cite> are removed, as they might represent extreme values (e.g. $$\gamma=0.005$$ in <d-cite key="shyu2003"></d-cite>). Afterwards, mean and correlation matrix are reestimated from the remaining observations.

**Principal components**\
Eigenvalues and eigenvectors are calculated via eigenvalue decomposition from the $$p \times p$$ correlation matrix $$\mathbf{R}$$. This is the covariance matrix of the standardized values $$\mathbf{z}$$. This yields $$p$$ pairs of the eigenvalue and unit eigenvector $$(\lambda_i, \mathbf{e}_i)$$, ordered such that $$\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots \geq \lambda_p$$.

The key quantity is the $$i$$-th principal component score $$y_i$$. It is calculated by projecting each standardized observation $$\mathbf{z}$$ onto the $$i$$-th eigenvector $$\mathbf{e}_i$$:

<p>
$$y_i = \mathbf{e}_i^\top \mathbf{z} = e_{i1} z_1 + e_{i2} z_2 + \dots + e_{ip} z_p $$
</p>

The score $$y_i$$ has variance $$\lambda_i$$ on the training data, so $$y_i^2 / \lambda_i$$ represents the squared, variance-normalized distance from the origin along the $$i$$-th principal axis. The sum of $$y_i^2/\lambda_i$$ over all $$p$$ components yields the squared Mahalanobis distance <d-cite key="shyu2003"></d-cite>, which is referred by Aggarwal <d-cite key="aggarwal2017"></d-cite> as *soft PCA*.

**Major and minor components**\
Instead of summing over all components, Shyu et al. evaluate the major and minor components using two separate statistics. 

- The major statistic sums over the first $$q$$ components, where $$q$$ is the smallest number of components that together explain more than $$50\%$$ of the total variance. It detects observations with extreme values along the dominant directions.

  <p>
  $$T_{\text{major}}(\mathbf{z}) = \sum_{i=1}^{q} \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}.$$
  </p>

- The minor statistic sums over the last $$r$$ components with eigenvalues $$\lambda_i < 0.20$$, where the weight $$1/\lambda_i$$ amplifies small deviations that violate the normal correlation structure.

  <p>
  $$T_{\text{minor}}(\mathbf{z}) = \sum_{i=p-r+1}^{p} \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}.$$
  </p>

The minor statistic resembles Aggarwal's *hard PCA* <d-cite key="aggarwal2017"></d-cite>, which scores observations only along low-variance components. By pairing it with $$T_{\text{major}}(\mathbf{z})$$, the PCC also captures extreme values along the dominant directions that hard PCA misses.

**Classification**\
To classify an unseen observation $$\mathbf{z}$$, anomaly thresholds $$c_1$$ and $$c_2$$ are defined for the major and minor statistics. An observation is flagged as anomalous if either threshold is exceeded:

<p>
$$T_{\text{major}}(\mathbf{z}) > c_1 \quad \text{or} \quad T_{\text{minor}}(\mathbf{z}) > c_2 .$$
</p>

The thresholds $$c_1$$ and $$c_2$$ are the $$(1 - \alpha_1)$$- and $$(1 - \alpha_2)$$-quantiles of the empirical distributions of the two statistics on the training data, chosen according to individual false alarm rates $$\alpha_1$$ and $$\alpha_2$$ to achieve an overall false alarm rate of $$\alpha = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 - \alpha_1 \alpha_2$$, assuming that the two statistics are independent. In contrast to a single combined score, the two statistics also indicate which type of anomaly an observation represents.

## PyOD

PyOD's PCA detector standardizes the data, fits scikit-learn's [PCA](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.decomposition.PCA.html) <d-cite key="scikitlearn"></d-cite>, and scores observations with the explained variance ratios as weights. This pipeline deviates from the principal component classifier of Shyu et al. in several steps. Most of these deviations are simplifications; the score, however, is a different function.

The version of PyOD's PCA is at this point in time `3.6.8` <d-cite key="pyodPCA368"></d-cite>.

<a id="pyods_pca"></a>
### Overview of the differences

| Step | Shyu et al. | PyOD (defaults) | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standardization | sample std. (`ddof=1`) | `StandardScaler` (`ddof=0`) | negligible |
| Decomposition | eigendecomposition of $$\mathbf{R}$$ | SVD of the standardized data | identical up to the sign of each component |
| Trimming | remove the $$\gamma \cdot n$$ most extreme observations ($$\gamma = 0.005$$) | none | less robust to outliers in the training data |
| Components | $$q$$ major and $$r$$ minor, evaluated separately | all $$p$$ components in a single sum | major and minor anomalies are not distinguished |
| Threshold | $$c_1$$, $$c_2$$ from false alarm rates $$\alpha_1$$, $$\alpha_2$$ | one quantile of the training scores (`contamination=0.1`) | a single threshold |
| Score | $$\sum_i \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}$$ per statistic| $$p \sum_i \frac{\lVert \mathbf{z} - s_i \mathbf{e}_i \rVert}{\lambda_i}$$ | different function (see below) |

**Standardization**\
The `StandardScaler` <d-cite key="standardscaler"></d-cite> divides by the biased standard deviation (`ddof=0`). According to the documentation this choice is unlikely to affect the model performance.

**Decomposition**
scikit-learn, which is used by PyOD, computes the components via a singular value decomposition of the standardized data instead of an eigendecomposition of the correlation matrix. Both yield the same components, but only up to their sign: if $$\mathbf{e}_i$$ is a unit eigenvector of $$\mathbf{R}$$, so is $$-\mathbf{e}_i$$. Implementations therefore fix the sign by convention, such as `svd_flip` in scikit-learn <d-cite key="sklearnPCA"></d-cite> and the resulting sign has no meaningful interpretation in terms of the dat <d-cite key="broResolvingSignAmbiguity2008"></d-cite>. For Shyu's score this is irrelevant, because $$y_i$$ enters the statistic squared. For PyOD's score it is not, as we show below.

**Trimming**\
PyOD fits the PCA on all training observations. Outliers in nominally normal training data can inflate the estimated variances and rotate the components <d-cite key="shyu2003"></d-cite>, so that anomalies partly become part of the learned normal subspace <d-cite key="ringbergSensitivityPCATraffic2007a"></d-cite>. Shyu et al. prevent this by trimming.

**Component Selection**\
By default, PyOD uses all $$p$$ components. If `n_components` is set to $$k < p$$, only the $$k$$ components with the largest variance are retained. `n_selected_components` then selects the *last* of these for scoring. They correspond to minor components in the sense of Shyu et al. only if $$k = p$$. PyOD provides no option to evaluate major and minor components separately.

### Score Calculation

With the default parameters, PyOD computes the score as follows <d-cite key="pyodPCA368"></d-cite>:

```python
# fit: learned from the standardized training data
self.selected_components_ = self.detector_.components_
self.selected_w_components_ = self.detector_.explained_variance_ratio_
# decision_function: X = standardized observations
np.sum(cdist(X, self.selected_components_) / self.selected_w_components_, axis=1)
```

| Code | Notation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `X` | rows $$\mathbf{z}_j$$, shape $$n \times p$$ | standardized observations |
| `selected_components_` | rows $$s_i\mathbf{e}_i$$, shape $$p \times p$$ | unit eigenvectors, sign $$s_i \in \{-1,+1\}$$ set by `svd_flip` |
| `selected_w_components_` | $$\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j = \lambda_i / p$$ | explained variance ratios, used as weights |
| `cdist(...)` | $$\lVert \mathbf{z}_j - s_i\mathbf{e}_i \rVert$$, shape $$n \times p$$ | Euclidean distance from each observation to each eigenvector *tip* (a point, not the axis) |
| `/ ...`, `np.sum(..., axis=1)` | $$\sum_i \cdot \, p/\lambda_i$$ | weight column $$i$$ by $$p/\lambda_i$$, sum per observation |

The score is therefore

<p>
$$
\text{score}_{\text{PyOD}}(\mathbf{z}) = p \sum_{i=1}^{p} \frac{\lVert \mathbf{z} - s_i\, \mathbf{e}_i \rVert}{\lambda_i},
\qquad s_i \in \{-1, +1\}.
$$
</p>

The code comment introduced in commit [a6f05f3b](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/commit/a6f05f3be9cddeea2e5a57f6c9c7cbb05efd9723) describes exactly this computation. It was later replaced by the references to Shyu et al. and Aggarwal cited in the documentation, while the code remained unchanged. The implemented score deviates from these references in two fundamental ways.

<a id="problem-1"></a>
#### Problem 1: The sign

Expanding the numerator (Appendix 1) gives
 
<p>
$$
\lVert \mathbf{z} - s_i \mathbf{e}_i \rVert = \sqrt{\lVert \mathbf{z}\rVert^2 - 2\, s_i\, y_i + 1}.
$$
</p>

Shyu's score contains $$y_i^2$$, so the sign of $$\mathbf{e}_i$$ cancels out. In PyOD's $$s_i y_i$$ the sign matters. Flipping it changes the scores and can therfore change the ranking of observations, as already reported in issue #217 <d-cite key="pyodIssue217"></d-cite>.

**Consequences for anomalies**\
Each term of PyOD's score measures how far a sample is from the tip of an eigenvector, $$s_i \mathbf{e}_i$$, and this tip lies on only one side of the center. Consider two observations that deviate from the center by the same amount along $$\mathbf{e}_i$$, but in opposite directions. The sample on the side of the tip moves closer to it, so its term shrinks. The sample on the other side moves away from it, so its term grows. For Shyu et al., both observations are equally anomalous. For PyOD, one of them looks less anomalous, and which one is decided by the sign convention, not by the data.

The effect is strongest for the minor components, because their terms carry the largest weights $$p/\lambda_i$$. In the extreme case, a sample lies exactly at the tip, $$\mathbf{z} = s_i \mathbf{e}_i$$. For Shyu et al., this is a clear anomaly with score $$1/\lambda_i$$. For PyOD, its distance to the tip is zero, so exactly the term with the largest weight vanishes.

<a id="problem-2"></a>
#### Problem 2: The score is close to the norm of $$\mathbf{z}$$

Each term of the score is the distance from $$\mathbf{z}$$ to an eigenvector tip $$s_i \mathbf{e}_i$$, multiplied by the weight $$p/\lambda_i$$. All tips lie at distance 1 from the center, because they are unit vectors. The distance from $$\mathbf{z}$$ to a tip is therefore almost the same as the distance from $$\mathbf{z}$$ to the center: by the triangle inequality, it is at least $$\lVert \mathbf{z}\rVert - 1$$ and at most $$\lVert \mathbf{z}\rVert + 1$$. Every term is thus roughly $$\lVert \mathbf{z}\rVert$$ times its weight, and the whole score is roughly

<p>
$$
\text{score}_{\text{PyOD}}(\mathbf{z}) \approx W \cdot \lVert \mathbf{z}\rVert, \qquad W = p\sum_{i=1}^{p} \frac{1}{\lambda_i}.
$$
</p>

$$W$$ depends only on the eigenvalues of the training data and is the same for every sample. The score is therefore essentially the distance to the center, multiplied by a constant. The direction in which a sample deviates can shift its score by at most $$\pm W$$, i.e. by as much as one unit of norm would.

**Eigenvalue problematic**\
The key difference lies in when the eigenvalues enter the score. Shyu et al. first scale each direction separately and then measure the distance, so each component score $$y_i$$ is divided by $$\sqrt{\lambda_i}$$. That means a small step along a minor component counts as much as a large step along a major one. PyOD does it the other way round. It first computes the Euclidean distance to the tip $$s_i \mathbf{e}_i$$, in which all directions count equally, and only afterwards divides by $$\lambda_i$$. This division scales the whole distance by the same factor, no matter in which direction the sample deviates. The information about which directions are normal is therefore lost before the eigenvalue is even applied.

The norm ignores how the features are correlated, which is exactly the structure PCA is meant to capture. Correlation-breaking anomalies that are not unusually far from the center are therefore hard to detect. The effect grows with the dimension: for standardized data, $$\mathbb{E}\lVert \mathbf{z}\rVert^2 = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{R}) = p$$, so on average $$\lVert \mathbf{z}\rVert \approx \sqrt{p}$$ and the tolerance of $$\pm 1$$ shrinks relative to the norm. This matches issue #64 <d-cite key="pyodIssue64"></d-cite>, where the PCA scores correlated at 0.99 with the distance to the origin. 

Geometrically speaking, Shyu's contours of equal score are ellipses, stretched along the major and squeezed along the minor components. PyOD's terms have circular contours around the eigenvector tips, which treat all directions alike. Away from the center, their sum looks like circles around the center.

### A short example
 
Consider $$p = 2$$ standardized sensors with correlation $$\rho = 0.95$$, so that $$\lambda_1 = 1.95$$, $$\mathbf{e}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1, 1)$$ and $$\lambda_2 = 0.05$$, $$\mathbf{e}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1, -1)$$. In Shyu's classifier, $$q = r = 1$$. We score a few observations with Shyu's statistics and with PyOD under both signs of $$\mathbf{e}_2$$:
 
| Observation | | Shyu | PyOD, $$s_2 = +1$$ | PyOD, $$s_2 = -1$$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $$0$$ | training mean | 0.00 | 41.03 | 41.03 |
| $$A = (2, 2)$$ | follows the correlation | 4.10 | 121.88 | 121.88 |
| $$B = (1.5, -1.5)$$ | breaks the correlation | 90.00 | 47.26 | 127.26 |
| $$\mathbf{e}_2$$ | tip of the minor eigenvector | 20.00 | 1.45 | 81.45 |
| $$-\mathbf{e}_2$$ | its mirror image | 20.00 | 81.45 | 1.45 |
 
For Shyu et al., $$B$$ is by far the most anomalous sample, and $$A$$ is unremarkable. PyOD with $$s_2 = +1$$ ranks $$A$$ well above $$B$$. Flipping the sign of $$\mathbf{e}_2$$ reverses the order of $$A$$ and $$B$$ and swaps the scores of the two tips. Whichever tip coincides with $$s_2 \mathbf{e}_2$$ scores lower than the training mean itself.

## Community reports
The community has reported the problem several times over the past years. The following timeline summarizes the relevant issues and pull requests.

In 2019 a user observed on his data that the PCA scores correlate with the Euclidean distance to the origin ($$r = 0.99$$). The maintainer closed the issue, attributing this to the data <d-cite key="pyodIssue64"></d-cite>.

In 2020 pull request #211 <d-cite key="pyodPR211"></d-cite> implemented the correct projection, but normalized the squared scores by the explained variance ratio instead of the eigenvalue $$\lambda_i$$. This scales all scores by the total variance $$\sum_j \lambda_j$$, preserving the exact Mahalanobis ranking while changing the numerical values. With the default `whiten=False`, the proposal in Issue #217 <d-cite key="pyodIssue217"></d-cite> is equivalent to #211. Enabling `whiten=True` causes `fit_transform` to return already normalized scores $$t_i = y_i / \sqrt{\lambda_i}$$, so each component is weighted by $$1/\lambda_i^2$$ instead of $$1/\lambda_i$$.

In 2021, pull request #340 <d-cite key="pyodPR340"></d-cite> took up the idea of #217, but centers with the mean of the current batch instead of the training mean. As a result, a test sample scored on its own always gets a score of 0. In addition, the weight is placed inside the square, so the components are weighted by $$1/\lambda_i^2$$ instead of $$1/\lambda_i$$. 

None of these proposals has been merged to date. The following table summarizes the scores.

|version | score | 
|---|---|
| current PyOD | $$\sum_i \frac{\lVert \mathbf{z} - \mathbf{e}_i \rVert_2}{\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j}$$|
| [#211](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/pull/211) | $$\sum_i \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j}$$|
| [#217](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/issues/217) | $$\sum_i \frac{t_i^2}{\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j}$$|
| [#340](https://github.com/yzhao062/pyod/pull/340) | $$\sum_i \left(\frac{t_i - \bar{t}_i^{\text{batch}}}{\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j}\right)^2$$ |
| ours | $$\sum_i \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}$$ |

where $$y_i = \mathbf{e}_i^\top \mathbf{z}$$ denotes the projection of the standardized observation $$\mathbf{z}$$ onto eigenvector $$\mathbf{e}_i$$, and $$t_i$$ denotes the output of sklearn's transform ($$t_i = y_i$$ for`whiten=False`, and $$t_i = y_i / \sqrt{\lambda_i}$$ for `whiten=True`)

## Suggested Fix
Our fix adopts the projection proposed in #211, but normalizes by the eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ instead of the explained variance ratios. Relative to PyOD 3.6.7, the change is:
```diff
- self.w_components_ = self.detector_.explained_variance_ratio_
+ self.w_components_ = self.detector_.explained_variance_
  ...
- return np.sum(cdist(X, self.selected_components_)
-               / self.selected_w_components_, axis=1).ravel()
+ projections = (X - self.detector_.mean_) @ self.selected_components_.T
+ return np.sum(projections ** 2 / self.selected_w_components_,
+               axis=1).ravel()
```
This resolves both problems. Because $$y_i$$ enters the statistic squared, the anomaly score is invariant to the sign of the eigenvectors ([Problem 1](#problem-1)). Normalizing each principal direction by its eigenvalue $$\lambda_i$$ ensures that the correlation structure is preserved ([Problem 2](#problem-2)). With the default parameters, the score equals the squared Mahalanobis distance, i.e., Aggarwal's soft PCA. If n_components $= p$ and n_selected_components $= r$, it corresponds to the minor statistic $T_{\text{minor}}$ of Shyu et al..

## Impact

Using the GitHub code search API, we searched for all import styles of PyOD’s PCA, such as from pyod.models.pca import PCA or import pyod.models.pca. On 10 November 2026, this returned 910 unique files in 525 repositories, excluding forks of PyOD. They range from small projects to widely used products like PostHog (40k stars) or TDengine (25k stars).

Two limitations apply to this number. First, 23 of the 67 papers are indexed with arXiv as their venue and only some of them were later published elsewhere. Second, the search identifies only papers whose own code imports or copies the implementation. A paper that copies PCA results from a benchmark such as ADBench reports affected numbers without importing PyOD. The 67 papers are therefore a lower bound on the number of publications that report affected results.

Even a dedicated search cannot determine the exact number of affected publications, and papers that copy benchmark results instead of importing PyOD remain invisible to it. Although the deviation had been public since 2019, ADBench adopted the detector as a baseline in 2022. There is currently no systematic way to answer the question: which published results depend on this code?

## From a single bug to the community
### Reproducible but not valid
ADBench's PCA results are reproducible. Rerunning the benchmark with the same PyOD version used at the time yields the same numbers. However, they were not computed with the method of Shyu et al. that PyOD cites. We therefore shouldn't only ask whether results are reproducible (Does the same code produce the same results?), but also whether they are valid (Do these results come from the method they are attributed to?).

Reproducibility can be checked if code, version numbers and data are available. Validity often becomes apparent only after publication when someone analyzes the code, finds a deviation from the references and reports it. Even if the bug is report, there isn't a guarantee that it will be fixed. If it gets fixed and the library yields different results from the corrected release on, results computed with earlier releases remain in the literature. 

In neither scenario does version information make a result valid. What version information does provide is a way to determine whether a published result is affected by a known problem.
### Why existing practices are not enough
Tracing a known problem to the results that are affected requires a record of which code and versions are affected, a record of which code and versions a result depends on and a way to link the two. Existing practices only provide parts og this. Pinned version record what a result depends on, but there is nothing to match them against. Changelogs are kind of a record of known problems, but they don't link a problem to specific functions and affected versions in a machine-readable way. They also only document changes that have been fixed, not those that are still open. Issues are scattered across repositories. Rerunning every result would make matching unnecessary, but it would be too expensive and time-consuming.

For security vulnerabilities the OSV schema records affected package versions in a machine-readable format and is exported by GitHub Security Advisories, the PyPI Advisory Database and others. Tools such as Renovate match a project's dependencies against these records automatically.
### A proposal for a community-wide solution
We propose four building blocks that rely on existing infrastructure and can be adopted independently. The first three provide the components identified above and the fourth ensures that a match reaches the people concerned.

**Provenance**
Repositories pin exact versions, for example in a lockfile. Papers state for each baseline whether it was computed (library and version) or copied (source). Copying results remains legitimate, but becomes traceable.

**Advisories**
We propose correctness advisories which are structured records analogous to OSV entries. An advisory describes the code:
```yaml
id: MLCA-2026-0001
package: pyod
affected: {function: pyod.models.pca.PCA.decision_function, versions: "<=3.6.8"}
type: deviation-from-cited-method
cited_method: [Shyu et al. 2003, Aggarwal 2017]
status: confirmed      # reported | confirmed | disputed | fixed
fixed_in: null
evidence: [issue 64, issue 217, PR 211, PR 340]
```
If maintainers contest an advisory, it is marked as disputed but remains visible.

A single advisory can affect many papers, which are identified at different times and with varying certainty. Each affected result is therefore recorded in its own entry with its own identifier, which refers to the advisory:
```yaml
id: MLCR-2026-0001
refers_to: MLCA-2026-0001   # the advisory above
result: ADBench (NeurIPS 2022), PCA results   # TODO: table numbers
status: confirmed      # candidate | confirmed | not-affected
evidence: benchmark code calls pyod.models.pca.PCA; results reported in Tables X–Y   # TODO
```
An entry starts as a candidate when an automated search finds that a paper's code depends on an affected version. It becomes confirmed or not-affected once someone checks the public code and paper. Advisories can be hosted in a public Git repository with one file per record, curated by a small group via pull requests.

Maintainers can contribute by adding a dedicated changelog category for changes that alter results for the same data and parameters, as scikit-learn already maintains <d-cite key="sklearn13changelog"></d-cite>. Its entries translate directly into advisories with fixed_in.

**Matching**
A tool checks a repository's dependencies against affected version ranges and a paper's bibliography against affected-result records. New advisories also trigger a reverse search for dependent repositories, as we performed manually in Impact.

**Visibility**
Warnings are most effective where researchers already work. The reference manager Zotero, for example, checks libraries against the Retraction Watch database, warns about retracted works already cited in a document and asks for confirmation before a retracted work is cited <d-cite key="zotero2019retraction"></d-cite>. Applied to our case, anyone citing ADBench to copy its PCA results would receive a warning. The check thus falls to the person copying the result, who has an incentive to get it right and needs to examine only that one paper. Reviewers can run the same check on a submission's bibliography. The published paper itself remains unchanged.

## Conclusion

PyOD’s PCA detector cites Shyu et al., but scores observations by a weighted sum of Euclidean distances to the eigenvector tips instead of by variance-normalized projections. This score depends on the arbitrary sign of each eigenvector and is approximately proportional to the distance from the center. The detector is therefore largely insensitive to anomalies that violate the correlation structure without lying far from the center, which are precisely the anomalies the minor components are meant to detect. The deviation has been reported since 2019, but none of the proposed fixes has been merged.

Replacing the distances with squared projections normalized by the eigenvalues makes the score sign-invariant and turns it into the squared Mahalanobis distance. The corrected detector is not uniformly better. On ADBench, ROC AUC and AP change substantially in both directions depending on the datasets. Our main finding is therefore not a performance gain. It is that the PCA results reported in ADBench, at least 67 papers and 525 repositories were obtained with a score other than the one described in the cited references.

### Future work
Our fix deliberately changes only the score, so that its effect on ADBench can be attributed to this single component. The remaining parts of the PCC, namely trimming, separate major and minor statistics, and two thresholds, are not implemented in PyOD. Such components can substantially change the behavior of a method. A natural next step is to implement the full PCC as a separate detector class and to evaluate its components one at a time. This requires an additional design decision, because the PCC is a classifier with two thresholds, whereas benchmarks such as ADBench evaluate a single continuous score.

### Final Thoughts

This post is not meant to blame the PyOD maintainers. A library of this reach is a huge service to the community, and we use it ourselves. Mistakes happen in every codebase, ours included. What surprised us is how long this one survived. It was reported publicly years ago, yet the detector continued to serve as a baseline in benchmarks and papers. This is not unique to PyOD. In deep reinforcement learning, Engstrom et al. <d-cite key="engstromImplementationMattersDeep2020"></d-cite> showed that implementation details not described in the paper account for much of the reported advantage of a popular algorithm over its predecessor. Popularity is not proof of correctness. A widely used library may even be checked less, because everyone assumes someone else already has.

Generative AI cuts both ways here. Code written by humans, mistakes included, becomes training data, so an implementation like this one can resurface in generated code <d-cite key="jesseLargeLanguageModels2023"></d-cite>. At the same time, comparing an implementation with its documentation or the paper it cites is exactly the kind of tedious task where LLMs can help. As Shah <d-cite key="researchAskingAuthorsTheir2026"></d-cite> reminds us, authors remain responsible for everything they publish, whether written by hand or with AI. We think this responsibility extends to the tools a paper builds on.

We therefore recommend: before you report results for a method, check that its implementation does what the paper says. Read the scoring function, look at the open issues, and test it on a toy example where you know the answer, like the [two-sensor example](#a-short-example) above. An LLM can do a first pass.

Norm as a dot product

<p>
$$\lVert \mathbf{z} - s_i \mathbf{e}_i \rVert^2 = (\mathbf{z} - s_i \mathbf{e}_i)'(\mathbf{z} - s_i \mathbf{e}_i)$$
</p>

Expand it, and as the dot product is symmetric, we can combine the two middle terms

<p>
$$(\mathbf{z} - s_i \mathbf{e}_i)'(\mathbf{z} - s_i \mathbf{e}_i) = \mathbf{z}'\mathbf{z} \;-\; s_i\, \mathbf{z}'\mathbf{e}_i \;-\; s_i\, \mathbf{e}_i'\mathbf{z} \;+\; s_i^2\, \mathbf{e}_i'\mathbf{e}_i = \mathbf{z}'\mathbf{z} \;-\; 2\, s_i\, \mathbf{e}_i'\mathbf{z} \;+\; s_i^2\, \mathbf{e}_i'\mathbf{e}_i$$
</p>

Now we substitute

| Term | Value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| $$\mathbf{z}'\mathbf{z}$$ | $$\lVert \mathbf{z} \rVert^2$$ | definition of the norm |
| $$\mathbf{e}_i'\mathbf{z}$$ | $$y_i$$ | Shyu et al., Eq. (4): $$y_i = \mathbf{e}_i'\mathbf{z}$$ |
| $$s_i^2$$ | $$1$$ | $$s_i \in \{-1, +1\}$$ |
| $$\mathbf{e}_i'\mathbf{e}_i$$ | $$1$$ | eigenvectors have unit length (`components_` is orthonormal) |

This gives:

<p>
$$\lVert \mathbf{z} - s_i \mathbf{e}_i \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{z} \rVert^2 - 2\,s_i\,y_i + 1$$
</p>

Take the square root

<p>
$$\lVert \mathbf{z} - s_i \mathbf{e}_i \rVert = \sqrt{\lVert \mathbf{z} \rVert^2 - 2\,s_i\,y_i + 1}$$
</p>
